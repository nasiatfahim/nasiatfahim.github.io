Statement

How I teach

I learned to program the way most of my future students will: by being wrong repeatedly, in public, against a clock. Fifteen hundred competitive-programming problems is mostly a record of failures, and the useful part of that record is knowing exactly where a beginner’s understanding tends to break.

My first commitment is to make the failure visible. A student who cannot explain why their solution is O(n²) has not learned complexity, however well they recite the definition. I prefer to build a lecture around a wrong answer — a solution that passes the sample and fails the fourth test case — and work outward from why, because the diagnosis is the transferable skill and the correct code is not.

My second commitment is to teach from real data. My own research runs on Bangladeshi seismic records, Sentinel‑1 imagery of the delta and protein sequences from local fish species, and I bring that material into teaching wherever a course allows it. A machine-learning class that only ever sees MNIST teaches students that data arrives clean, labelled and balanced. It does not.

My third commitment is to be honest about what models cannot do. My research is largely about the conditions under which a well-performing model quietly stops working. Students going into an industry that will ask them to deploy these systems should graduate able to ask that question unprompted.

Concretely, that means assessment weighted toward projects with real datasets and defended orally; office hours where I ask the student to explain the bug to me rather than the other way round; and course materials, slides and problem sets published openly on this site as I develop them.

Supervision

Thesis topics I can supervise

I would be glad to supervise undergraduate theses on applied machine learning, particularly where a student can work with locally relevant data rather than a downloaded benchmark. Some starting points:

  • Flood extent mapping over Bangladeshi rivers from Sentinel‑1, and how far a model trained on one basin transfers to another
  • Seismic signal processing and magnitude estimation from the Bangladesh–India regional network
  • Protein sequence classification for fisheries, food authentication or biodiversity monitoring
  • Bengali NLP — regional dialect speech recognition, low-resource language modelling
  • Explainability audits: taking a deployed or published model and characterising where it fails
  • Retrieval-augmented systems over Bengali document collections

If you are a student interested in any of these, write to me with a paragraph on what you find interesting about it.
